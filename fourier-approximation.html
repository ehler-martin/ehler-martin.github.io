Numerical harmonic analysis

Quantitative estimates: How well does the discrete Fourier transform approximate the Fourier transform on ℝ?

Imagine recording a single fading vibration. With just 24 readings, would you watch it for longer or take the readings closer together? The answer depends on what the signal does in time and in frequency.

Martin Ehler, Karlheinz Gröchenig & Andreas Klotz

SIAM Review 68 (1), 93–123 · 2026

Read the paper Open preprint

Where does the error come from?

The Fourier transform describes how much of each frequency a signal contains. Its definition uses the whole signal, extending indefinitely in both directions. A computer receives finitely many values. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) efficiently computes the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of those values. The paper asks how close that result is to the continuous transform at the same frequencies.

When the recording is too short

Imagine stopping the recording while the vibration is still audible. Whatever happens outside the window is missing from the calculation. In the experiment, choose Short and look at the part of the wave that extends beyond the shaded area. This is truncation.

When the readings are too far apart

Choose Wide. Now the recording lasts 12 seconds, with just two readings per second. On this grid, a 1.5 Hz oscillation gives the same readings as a 0.5 Hz oscillation. The computed peak moves inward, while the exact spectrum still peaks near ±1.5 Hz. This ambiguity is called aliasing.

How wide should the window be?

With a fixed number of readings, a wider window also means a coarser grid. The authors give error bounds that account for both effects. A signal with long tails needs more room. A signal with appreciable high-frequency content needs closer spacing.

For the pulse shown here, the error falls as the window first widens, then rises as aliasing takes over. A different signal can have a different best window.

The estimate and the experiment, in symbols

Write n for the sample count, h for the spacing, and p = nh for the window length. For suitable polynomial decay of both the signal and its Fourier transform, Theorem 2.1 gives a bound of the form

E ≤ C (p−α + hβ).

The exponent α reflects decay in time and β reflects decay in frequency. Balancing the two terms leads to a spacing proportional to n−α/(α+β). Equal exponents give the familiar inverse-square-root spacing. The paper derives choices for unequal exponents as well.

Our illustration uses f(t) = e−πt² cos(3πt). With frequency measured in cycles per second, its transform is the average of two unit Gaussians centered at −1.5 and +1.5 Hz. We compute the scaled DFT directly at frequencies k/p, using the paper’s convention −n/2 < j, k ≤ n/2.
